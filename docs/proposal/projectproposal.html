D1 Project Planning

1. Project Overview

Project title: Benchmarking and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning of Adult Facial Expression Recognition Models on Child Expressions.

This project investigates how well facial expression recognition (FER) models trained on adult faces transfer to children, and whether parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) can close the developmental domain gap at a small computational and data cost. The study uses the public RAF-DB adult benchmark and the Child Affective Facial Expression (CAFE) set. It compares a convolutional backbone (ResNet-50) and a vision transformer backbone (ViT-B/16) under three adaptation conditions: linear probing, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), and full fine-tuning. A second stage introduces an age-aware adapter trained with a class-conditional domain-adversarial loss to test whether age-related features can be partially decoupled from emotion features without removing emotion-relevant information.

The project is an evaluation and method-development study. It is not a clinical diagnostic system, a deployed application, or a claim that the CAFE results generalise to all children. Its main outputs are a reproducible cross-age benchmark, evidence on the accuracy–efficiency trade-off of PEFT, and a bounded test of age-adversarial adaptation.

2. Background and Motivation

FER has advanced rapidly on adult benchmarks such as RAF-DB (Li et al., 2017), AffectNet (Mollahosseini et al., 2019), CK+ (Lucey et al., 2010) and FER2013 (Goodfellow et al., 2013). Modern CNN and transformer models can learn discriminative facial features, and architectures such as POSTER++ combine landmark and image information for strong adult benchmark performance (Mao et al., 2025). However, high adult accuracy does not establish transfer to children. Children differ in craniofacial proportions, facial fat distribution, muscle development, expression intensity and the contexts in which expressions occur. Child-labelled data are also scarce because of ethical, legal and practical constraints.

CAFE is one of the few standardised child FER resources. It contains 1,192 photographs of 154 children aged 2–8 years posing seven expressions (LoBue & Thrasher, 2015). The images are frontal, captured against a standard background, and copyright-protected. CAFE is therefore useful for controlled comparison but is not a spontaneous or infant dataset. This distinction matters: the present project studies children aged 2–8, not infants, and its conclusions must be limited to the CAFE setting.

Full fine-tuning of a large pretrained backbone on a small child set can overfit and requires more memory and storage than a student project may afford. PEFT freezes most pretrained weights and trains a small set of additional parameters. Adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019) and LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) are established in natural language processing and are increasingly used in FER (Saadi et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025). Their value for adult-to-child FER has not, to the best of our knowledge, been systematically benchmarked. This project addresses that gap.

3. Problem Statement

Problem 1 — Unquantified cross-age degradation. It is known that adult-trained FER models can degrade on child faces (Zheng et al., 2019; Witherow et al., 2019), but the size and class-specific pattern of the degradation are not well established for modern ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 backbones. Aggregate accuracy can conceal poor recall on difficult classes such as fear, sadness and surprise.

Problem 2 — Unclear PEFT trade-off under child data scarcity. Full fine-tuning, LoRA and linear probing have different capacity, memory and overfitting behaviour. The reviewed literature does not establish whether LoRA matches full fine-tuning on CAFE, especially in low-data regimes, or whether any gain is concentrated in majority classes.

Problem 3 — Age–emotion entanglement. Age-related morphology may be correlated with expression cues. A domain-adversarial loss can remove age information, but an unconditional adversary may also remove emotion-relevant information. The project must test whether a class-conditional adapter improves child recognition without degrading adult recognition.

Problem 4 — Evaluation confounds. RAF-DB and CAFE differ in capture conditions, pose, background, label distribution and population. A cross-dataset comparison therefore confounds age with dataset shift. The project will report this limitation, use subject-disjoint splits, and optionally add a posed adult control (CK+) to separate age from capture conditions more carefully.

Problem 5 — Reproducibility. Child data access is restricted, CAFE images cannot be redistributed, and small-sample results are sensitive to splits and seeds. The project requires documented data versions, subject-disjoint folds, fixed seeds, and traceable experiment logs.

4. Research Questions

RQ1. To what extent do adult-trained ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 models degrade on child facial expressions in CAFE, and which emotion classes are most affected?

RQ2. How does LoRA compare with full fine-tuning and linear probing in child FER accuracy, minority-class recall and parameter efficiency under full-data and low-data regimes?

RQ3. Can an age-aware adapter with a class-conditional domain-adversarial loss improve child expression recognition and reduce age-related feature entanglement without degrading adult performance?

5. Objectives

  1. To construct a reproducible RAF-DB-to-CAFE benchmark with subject-disjoint CAFE splits and to evaluate ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 under zero-shot and adapted conditions.
  2. To implement and compare linear probing, LoRA and full fine-tuning on child FER, reporting accuracy, macro-F1, unweighted average recall (UAR), per-class recall, calibration, trainable parameters, memory and training time.
  3. To design and evaluate an age-aware adapter with a class-conditional domain-adversarial loss, using emotion accuracy, age-probe accuracy and attention maps to assess whether age-related features are reduced without harming emotion recognition.

6.1 Data and task

The project uses RAF-DB as the adult source and CAFE as the child target. RAF-DB provides 29,672 real-world images, including 15,339 images in the seven-class basic-emotion subset with an official 12,271/3,068 train–test split (Li et al., 2017). CAFE provides 1,192 photographs of 154 children aged 2–8 years across seven classes (LoBue & Thrasher, 2015). CAFE access requires Databrary authorisation, and the images are copyright-protected.

An optional CK+ control may be added. CK+ is a lab-controlled adult dataset with 593 sequences from 123 subjects (Lucey et al., 2010). Comparing CK+→CAFE with RAF-DB→CAFE helps separate age-related shift from capture-condition shift, although it does not eliminate the confound.

6.2 Models and adaptation

The core models are ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016) and ViT-B/16 (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), initialised with ImageNet weights. The adaptation conditions are linear probing, LoRA and full fine-tuning. LoRA will be implemented with the HuggingFace PEFT library where supported (Mangrulkar et al., 2022); a custom LoRA wrapper may be needed for convolutional layers in ResNet-50 because LoRA was originally designed for linear layers. The age-aware adapter is a bottleneck adapter with a gradient-reversal domain classifier. POSTER++ (Mao et al., 2025) is an optional external comparison, not a core dependency.

6.3 Evaluation and claims

Evaluation is cross-dataset and subject-disjoint. The project reports accuracy, macro-F1, UAR, per-class recall, expected calibration error, trainable parameter percentage, peak GPU memory and training time, with bootstrap confidence intervals and multiple seeds. Results are conditional on CAFE and RAF-DB; no clinical, causal or deployment claims will be made.

7. Preliminary Methodology

7.1 Data preparation

  • Download RAF-DB under its terms and record the version, checksum and split.
  • Apply for CAFE access through Databrary and record the authorisation date and usage conditions.
  • Detect and align faces (MTCNN or RetinaFace), resize to 224×224, and apply consistent augmentation: random horizontal flip, small rotation, colour jitter and random resized crop for training; deterministic resize and centre crop for evaluation.
  • Create subject-disjoint CAFE folds using child identity, stratified by emotion and age bin. No child may appear in both training and test folds.
  • Use the full CAFE set as the primary benchmark and record any excluded images. As a sensitivity analysis, optionally repeat the key comparison on the 709-image mouth-closed subset used by Witherow et al. (2019) to support direct comparison with that line of work.

7.2 Models and training

  • Adult source training: train ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 on RAF-DB, using the age metadata to exclude child images where possible and reporting the resulting age distribution. Use class-balanced cross-entropy or class weights, AdamW, mixed precision, early stopping and a fixed seed set.
  • Linear probe: freeze the backbone and train a linear classifier on CAFE features.
  • LoRA: freeze the backbone; inject low-rank updates into attention and MLP projections of ViT-B/16; try ranks 4, 8 and 16 with alpha = 2r and dropout 0.05. For ResNet-50, apply LoRA to linear and 1×1 convolutional layers if time permits; otherwise use full fine-tuning as the CNN adaptation baseline.
  • Full fine-tuning: update all parameters with a small learning rate and early stopping. On a 12 GB GPU, use batch size 8–16 with gradient accumulation and gradient checkpointing for ViT-B/16.
  • Low-data regimes: 1, 5, 10 and 20 shots per class from the CAFE training folds, repeated across folds and seeds.

7.3 Age-aware adapter

A bottleneck adapter is inserted after each ViT block: a down-projection from 768 to 64 dimensions, a GELU non-linearity, an up-projection back to 768, and a residual connection. A domain classifier is attached to the [CLS] representation through a gradient-reversal layer (Ganin et al., 2016). The domain label is adult vs child, and a second variant uses CAFE age bins (2–4, 5–6, 7–8). The training objective is:

L = L_emotion + λ · L_domain

To reduce the risk of removing emotion-relevant age cues, a class-conditional variant feeds the predicted emotion distribution (or the true emotion label during training) into the domain classifier. The project will compare LoRA-only, LoRA+adapter without GRL, LoRA+adapter+GRL and full fine-tuning+GRL. The loss weight λ will be selected on a validation fold, not on the test fold.

7.4 Evaluation and analysis

  • Classification: accuracy, macro-F1, UAR, per-class recall (especially fear, sadness and surprise), confusion matrices and expected calibration error (Guo et al., 2017).
  • Efficiency: trainable parameter percentage, peak GPU memory, training time per epoch and inference latency.
  • Entanglement: train an age probe on frozen features before and after adaptation. A successful adapter should reduce age-probe accuracy while preserving or improving emotion accuracy.
  • Attribution: Grad-CAM for ResNet-50 (Selvaraju et al., 2017) and attention rollout for ViT-B/16. Compare attention on adult and child faces and check whether it remains on face regions rather than background.
  • Uncertainty: bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over subject-disjoint folds; report mean ± standard deviation across seeds.

7.5 Reproducibility

All experiments will be logged in Weights & Biases with the configuration, seed, metrics, checkpoints and system information. Code will be maintained in a private GitHub repository with a README, requirements file and configuration files. Dataset files will not be committed; access instructions and checksums will be documented instead.

D2 Literature Review — Chapters 1 and 2

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Background of the Study

Facial expression recognition (FER) is the automatic inference of affective state from facial configurations. Research usually treats expressions as one of seven basic categories: anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise and neutral. Adult benchmarks such as RAF-DB (Li et al., 2017), AffectNet (Mollahosseini et al., 2019), CK+ (Lucey et al., 2010) and FER2013 (Goodfellow et al., 2013) have supported substantial progress. Deep convolutional networks such as ResNet (He et al., 2016) and vision transformers such as ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) can learn discriminative representations from these datasets, and landmark-aware architectures such as POSTER++ combine facial structure with image features (Mao et al., 2025).

This progress is uneven across age groups. Children's faces differ from adults' in craniofacial proportions, facial fat, muscle development and the intensity and timing of expressions. Child-labelled FER data are scarce because of ethical, legal and practical constraints. The Child Affective Facial Expression (CAFE) set is one of the few standardised resources: 1,192 photographs of 154 children aged 2–8 years, posing seven expressions against a consistent background (LoBue & Thrasher, 2015). CAFE is posed and does not include infants or spontaneous expressions, so it supports a controlled but bounded study.

Evidence from adult-to-child transfer is consistent with a developmental domain gap. Zheng et al. (2019) found that classifiers trained on child data were more accurate on child data than on adult data, and adult-trained classifiers were more accurate on adult data than on child data; within the same group, classifiers generally performed better for adults than for children. Witherow et al. (2019) reported that a model trained on CK+ and fine-tuned on a mouth-closed subset of CAFE reached 76.03% average 10-fold person-independent accuracy, and that adult-only training generalised poorly. Witherow et al. (2022) later reported that landmark fusion improved 10-fold cross-validation mean accuracy from 80.32% to 83.72%, and that fine-tuning with landmark fusion reached 85.14%. Witherow et al. (2024) proposed FACE-BE-SELF, a domain-adaptation method that aligns adult and child expression distributions and outperformed transfer learning and baseline domain-adaptation methods in their evaluation. Poyiadzi et al. (2021) studied age-group domain generalisation and found that class-conditional domain-adversarial neural networks (CDANN) performed best among the compared algorithms, while increasing the number of training age groups tended to improve performance on unseen age groups. Jannat and Canavan (2021) proposed a Siamese-network approach for expression recognition across age and reported comparable or better performance on EmoReact and ElderReact. Lee et al. (2024) proposed a child-centric source-domain reconstructor using a secondary diffusion model and a child emotion feature-guided classifier for domain-agnostic child FER.

These studies establish that adult-child adaptation is feasible, but they do not answer the parameter-efficiency question. Full fine-tuning updates all weights and can overfit a small child set; linear probing is cheap but may underfit. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) offers a middle path. Adapters insert small trainable modules into a frozen backbone (Houlsby et al., 2019), while Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) learns low-rank updates to existing weight matrices (Hu et al., 2022). PEFT has been applied to FER: PE-CLIP adapts CLIP with temporal and shared adapters and reports competitive dynamic FER performance with less than 6% of total parameters (Saadi et al., 2025); FineCLIPER uses adapters and multi-modal cues for dynamic FER (Chen et al., 2024); HLoRA-MER uses high-level LoRA for micro-expression recognition with 197k trainable parameters (Shao et al., 2025); and Du et al. (2025) report that progressive LoRA outperformed full fine-tuning and single-stage LoRA when training across RAF-DB, FER-Plus and AffectNet. However, these studies focus on adult or dynamic FER. A systematic adult-to-child PEFT benchmark remains missing.

Age-aware adaptation adds a second question. A domain-adversarial loss can encourage features that are invariant to age (Ganin et al., 2016), but age and emotion are entangled: facial features that reveal age may also carry expression information. An unconditional adversary may remove useful emotion cues. This project therefore tests a class-conditional age-aware adapter and evaluates it with both emotion accuracy and an age probe. The project title is Benchmarking and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning of Adult Facial Expression Recognition Models on Child Expressions. It studies children aged 2–8 years, not infants, and it does not claim clinical or deployment validity.

1.2 Problem Statement

Cross-age degradation is not well quantified for modern backbones. Prior work shows that adult-trained models can fail on children (Zheng et al., 2019; Witherow et al., 2019), but the size and class-specific pattern of the degradation are not well established for ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16. Aggregate accuracy can hide poor recall on fear, sadness and surprise, which are often the difficult classes in FER.

The PEFT trade-off under child data scarcity is unclear. Full fine-tuning, LoRA and linear probing differ in capacity, memory and overfitting behaviour. CAFE is small (1,192 images), so full fine-tuning may overfit, while linear probing may underfit. It is not known whether LoRA matches full fine-tuning on child FER, whether gains survive in low-data regimes, and whether any gain is concentrated in majority classes.

Age and emotion features may be entangled. A domain-adversarial loss can remove age information, but an unconditional adversary may also remove emotion-relevant information. The project must test whether a class-conditional adapter improves child recognition without degrading adult recognition, and whether an age probe confirms reduced age predictability.

Evaluation is confounded. RAF-DB and CAFE differ in capture conditions, pose, background, label distribution and population. A cross-dataset comparison therefore confounds age with dataset shift. CAFE is also posed and small. The project will use subject-disjoint splits, report confidence intervals, and optionally add CK+ as a posed adult control. It will not claim that an observed difference is caused by age alone.

Reproducibility is constrained. CAFE access is restricted, images cannot be redistributed, and small-sample results are sensitive to splits and seeds. The project requires documented data versions, subject-disjoint folds, fixed seeds and traceable experiment logs.

1.6 Significance and Expected Contribution

The expected contribution is a reproducible evaluation of adult-to-child FER under a controlled PEFT comparison, plus a bounded test of age-adversarial adaptation. The benchmark will show how much performance is lost on CAFE, which classes are most affected, and whether LoRA can match full fine-tuning with far fewer trainable parameters. The work is relevant to child-facing affective computing, fairness across age groups and resource-constrained model adaptation. It is an evaluation and method-development contribution, not a new FER algorithm or a deployed system.

Deliverables include the data and split specification, experiment configurations, trained checkpoints, evaluation tables, attention and calibration analyses, and a reproducible code repository. Clinical effectiveness, transfer to other child populations and real-world deployment remain untested.

1.7 Organisation of the Thesis

Chapter 1 establishes the problem, questions, objectives and scope. Chapter 2 reviews related work on FER datasets, adult-to-child transfer, PEFT, domain-adversarial training and evaluation. Later chapters will describe the methodology, implementation, results and discussion. This D2 submission contains the first two chapters and the reference list.

Chapter 2: Literature Review

2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews published work on facial expression recognition, adult-to-child transfer, parameter-efficient fine-tuning and age-aware domain adaptation. The review examines how dataset design, adaptation method and evaluation protocol affect the meaning of reported performance. It then identifies a bounded project contribution and connects the literature to the objectives in Chapter 1. Results reported in this chapter belong to the cited studies; they are not results of the proposed project.

2.2 Review Approach

The review uses a focused narrative approach. Searches combined terms such as facial expression recognition, child facial expressions, adult-child transfer, domain adaptation, domain generalisation, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, LoRA, adapter, age-invariant representation and CAFE. Sources included IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Springer, Frontiers, Nature and arXiv. The selection prioritised original studies that report child FER, adult-to-child adaptation, PEFT for FER, or domain-adversarial adaptation. Dataset papers and benchmark papers were included where they define the evaluation setting. The review is selective rather than an exhaustive systematic review. It does not claim a comprehensive database search count, a screening flow or a pooled estimate. Differences in datasets, label definitions and validation procedures prevent a defensible numerical ranking of all studies.

2.3 FER Tasks, Labels and Datasets

2.3.1 Task definitions

FER can be treated as static image classification or dynamic video classification. It can use categorical labels (basic emotions) or dimensional labels (valence and arousal). This project uses static images and seven categorical labels. The choice matters because dynamic datasets capture temporal dynamics that static models cannot use, while categorical labels simplify evaluation but may hide label ambiguity. Posed datasets provide controlled expressions; spontaneous datasets provide ecological validity but noisier labels. CAFE is posed, RAF-DB is in-the-wild, and CK+ is lab-controlled. These differences are part of the domain shift.

2.3.2 Adult benchmarks

RAF-DB contains 29,672 real-world images, including a seven-class basic-emotion subset of 15,339 images with an official 12,271/3,068 split (Li et al., 2017). It also provides age-range, gender and race attributes, which makes age-stratified analysis possible, although the dataset is not exclusively adult. AffectNet contains more than one million images, with approximately 450,000 manually annotated for eight basic expressions (Mollahosseini et al., 2019). CK+ contains 593 sequences from 123 subjects and is lab-controlled (Lucey et al., 2010). FER2013 contains 35,887 grayscale 48×48 images (Goodfellow et al., 2013). These datasets differ in size, label quality, capture conditions and class balance, so cross-dataset comparisons require care.

2.3.3 Child datasets

CAFE contains 1,192 photographs of 154 children aged 2–8 years, with seven expressions, a standard off-white background and overhead lighting (LoBue, 2014; LoBue & Thrasher, 2015). The images are copyright-protected and available through Databrary authorisation. CAFE-Ss provides two subsets of 140 photographs each, validated with 1,000 adults, for researchers who need smaller stimulus sets (Yang & LoBue, 2020). EmoReact is a multimodal dataset of children's emotional responses (Nojavanasghari et al., 2016). LIRIS-CSE contains spontaneous children's facial expressions (Khan et al., 2019). These resources are valuable but small, heterogeneous and often restricted. CAFE remains the most practical controlled target for this project, but its posed nature and narrow age range limit generalisation.

2.3.4 Cross-dataset confounds

A comparison between RAF-DB and CAFE changes age, capture conditions, pose, background, label distribution and population at the same time. An observed drop in accuracy cannot be attributed to age alone. A stronger design adds a posed adult control such as CK+ and compares CK+→CAFE with RAF-DB→CAFE. Even then, the datasets differ in label definitions and subject populations. The project will therefore describe the result as a cross-age, cross-dataset benchmark rather than a pure age effect.

2.4 Adult-to-Child Transfer and Age-Related Domain Shift

2.4.1 Evidence of degradation

Zheng et al. (2019) directly tested whether adult-tuned classifiers can be used for children. They found that classifiers trained on child data were more accurate on child data than on adult data, and adult-trained classifiers were more accurate on adult data than on child data; within the same group, classifiers generally performed better for adults than for children. Witherow et al. (2019) trained on CK+ and fine-tuned on a mouth-closed subset of CAFE. The best condition reached 76.03% average 10-fold person-independent accuracy, while adult-only training generalised poorly. These results support the existence of a developmental domain gap, but they also show that transfer learning can recover part of the loss.

2.4.2 Landmark fusion and domain adaptation

Witherow et al. (2022) fused facial landmark features with CNN features and reported that fusion improved 10-fold cross-validation mean accuracy from 80.32% to 83.72%; fine-tuning with landmark fusion reached 85.14%. Witherow et al. (2024) extended this line to FACE-BE-SELF, which aligns adult and child expression distributions in a shared latent space and selects landmark features using a mixture of Beta distributions. In their evaluation with 5-fold cross-validation on two adult-child dataset pairs, FACE-BE-SELF outperformed transfer learning and other baseline domain-adaptation methods. These studies show that facial structure and domain alignment are useful, but they do not test PEFT or modern transformer backbones.

2.4.3 Age-group domain generalisation

Poyiadzi et al. (2021) studied apparent emotional expression recognition across age groups. They compared domain-generalisation algorithms and found that class-conditional domain-adversarial neural networks (CDANN) performed best. They also observed that increasing the number of training age groups tended to improve performance on unseen age groups, and that excluding an age group affected neighbouring age groups more strongly. Jannat and Canavan (2021) proposed a Siamese-network approach that learns expression similarity relative to age and reported comparable or better performance on EmoReact and ElderReact. These studies support age-aware adaptation but focus on age groups rather than the specific adult-to-child setting.

2.4.4 Diffusion-based child FER

Lee et al. (2024) proposed a domain-agnostic child FER approach with a child-centric source-domain reconstructor and a child emotion feature-guided classifier. A secondary image diffusion model reconstructs images with childlike source-domain features while preserving emotion, and a cross-attention classifier uses the reconstructed features. The method was validated on public datasets. This work shows that generative reconstruction can address domain discrepancy, but it adds a heavy generative component and does not isolate the contribution of parameter-efficient adaptation.

2.4.5 Synthesis

The literature establishes that adult-child transfer is a real problem and that landmark fusion, domain adaptation and age-group generalisation can help. It does not provide a systematic comparison of LoRA, full fine-tuning and linear probing on child FER, nor does it test a class-conditional age-adversarial adapter with modern ViT and CNN backbones. Those are the gaps addressed here.

2.5 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

2.5.1 Adapters

Houlsby et al. (2019) introduced bottleneck adapters for parameter-efficient transfer learning in NLP. An adapter projects a hidden representation to a lower dimension, applies a non-linearity, and projects back to the original dimension, usually with a residual connection. The backbone is frozen and only the adapters and task head are trained. This design is attractive for small target datasets because it limits the number of trainable parameters and reduces catastrophic forgetting. The trade-off is that adapter placement, bottleneck size and training schedule affect performance.

2.5.2 LoRA

LoRA freezes the pretrained weight matrix and learns a low-rank update (Hu et al., 2022). For a weight matrix W, the update is W + BA, where B and A are low-rank matrices. The number of trainable parameters is controlled by the rank. LoRA can be merged into the base weights at inference, so it adds no latency after merging. It was developed for large language models but has been adapted to vision transformers and other architectures. Applying LoRA to convolutional layers requires a different implementation because the original method targets linear layers.

2.5.3 PEFT in FER

PE-CLIP adapts CLIP for dynamic FER with a temporal dynamic adapter and a shared adapter, and reports competitive performance on DFEW, FERV39K and AFEW while requiring less than 6% of total parameters (Saadi et al., 2025). FineCLIPER combines CLIP adapters with face segmentation, landmarks and multi-modal descriptions, and reports state-of-the-art dynamic FER results with few tunable parameters (Chen et al., 2024). HLoRA-MER applies high-level LoRA to a visual foundation model for micro-expression recognition and reports F1 scores of 84.24% on CASME II and 83.07% on SAMM with 197k trainable parameters (Shao et al., 2025). Du et al. (2025) studied cross-dataset generalisation of CLIP and SIGLIP on FER and proposed progressive LoRA; they report that progressive LoRA outperformed full fine-tuning and single-stage LoRA on RAF-DB, FER-Plus and AffectNet. These results show that PEFT is viable for FER, but the evidence is concentrated on adult, dynamic or micro-expression settings.

2.5.4 What remains unknown for adult-to-child FER

The reviewed literature does not establish whether LoRA matches full fine-tuning on a small child set, whether it improves minority-class recall, or whether its parameter efficiency comes at a cost in calibration or attention quality. Nor does it establish how LoRA interacts with age-adversarial adaptation. The project addresses these questions with a controlled benchmark and a small adapter.

2.6 Age-Aware Representation Learning and Domain-Adversarial Training

2.6.1 Domain-adversarial training

Domain-adversarial training adds a domain classifier and a gradient-reversal layer to a task network (Ganin et al., 2016). The task loss trains the features to predict emotion, while the domain loss trains the features to be indistinguishable across domains. The gradient-reversal layer makes the feature extractor maximise the domain loss. The result is a representation that is less informative about the domain. DANN is a standard baseline for unsupervised domain adaptation and has been used in cross-domain FER (Gao et al., 2024).

2.6.2 Class-conditional adaptation and age-invariant recognition

An unconditional domain classifier may remove information that is useful for emotion if that information also predicts age. Class-conditional DANN conditions the domain classifier on the emotion label or prediction, so that the adversary removes age information within each emotion class rather than across classes. Poyiadzi et al. (2021) found CDANN to be the best-performing domain-generalisation method in their age-group study. FACE-BE-SELF uses domain adaptation with landmark features and identity/expression/domain factor selection (Witherow et al., 2024). These ideas motivate a class-conditional age-aware adapter, but the risk of removing emotion cues remains.

2.6.3 Risks

Age-related features are not independent of expression. For example, facial proportions and skin texture may correlate with both age and expression intensity. A strong adversary may reduce age predictability while also reducing emotion accuracy. The project will therefore compare several λ values, use a class-conditional adversary, and evaluate both emotion accuracy and age-probe accuracy. A successful adapter should reduce age-probe accuracy without a significant drop in emotion accuracy; a failure is still informative.

2.6.4 Proposed design

The adapter is a bottleneck module inserted after each ViT block. The domain classifier is attached to the [CLS] representation through a gradient-reversal layer. The domain label is adult vs child, with a second variant using CAFE age bins. The loss is L = L_emotion + λ · L_domain. The project compares LoRA-only, LoRA+adapter without GRL, LoRA+adapter+GRL and full fine-tuning+GRL. The design is intentionally simple so that the effect of each component can be isolated.

2.7 Evaluation, Reproducibility and Fairness

2.7.1 Splits and leakage

CAFE contains multiple images per child. A random image split would place the same child in training and test sets and inflate performance. The project will use subject-disjoint folds, stratified by emotion and age. RAF-DB will use its official split. Preprocessing statistics will be fitted on training folds only. The test fold will not be used for hyperparameter selection or threshold tuning.

2.7.2 Metrics

Accuracy alone is insufficient when classes are imbalanced or when difficult classes matter. The project will report macro-F1, UAR, per-class recall, confusion matrices and expected calibration error (Guo et al., 2017). UAR gives each class equal weight and is useful when fear, sadness and surprise are rare. Calibration is relevant because a model can be accurate but overconfident, which matters for any downstream use.

2.7.3 Uncertainty and seeds

CAFE is small, so a single split can produce misleading results. The project will use 5-fold subject-disjoint cross-validation, at least two seeds per configuration, and bootstrap confidence intervals. Results will be reported as mean ± standard deviation. A difference will not be treated as meaningful unless it is consistent across folds and seeds.

2.7.4 Reproducibility and data access

CAFE is available through Databrary and is copyright-protected. The project will record the access date, dataset version, checksums and split files. Images will not be committed to the repository. Code, configurations and W&B run identifiers will be recorded so that each table can be traced to a run. RAF-DB will be used under its own terms.

2.7.5 Fairness

CAFE includes demographic metadata and a diverse child sample, but the sample is small. The project will report subgroup results by age, gender and race where sample sizes permit, and will avoid strong fairness conclusions from small cells. The goal is to identify whether adaptation helps or harms particular subgroups, not to certify fairness.

2.9 Synthesis and Project Position

Three conclusions follow from the review. First, the adult-to-child gap is supported by multiple studies, but the evidence is often confounded by dataset differences, small samples and posed expressions. A credible project must report these limitations rather than attribute all degradation to age. Second, PEFT is a promising way to adapt large backbones to small child data, but the existing FER evidence is concentrated on adult, dynamic or micro-expression settings. The reviewed literature does not report a systematic comparison of LoRA, full fine-tuning and linear probing on child FER. Third, age-adversarial adaptation can reduce age predictability, but an unconditional adversary may remove emotion-relevant information. A class-conditional adapter and an age probe are needed to test the trade-off.

The project does not claim that prior work lacks adult-child adaptation or PEFT for FER. Instead, it combines a bounded cross-age benchmark with a controlled PEFT comparison and a small age-aware adapter. The research gap addressed here is the need to establish, for RAF-DB and CAFE under a common protocol, which adaptation strategy is most accurate and parameter-efficient, and whether age-adversarial adaptation helps or harms emotion recognition. This is an evaluation and method-development contribution, not a new clinical prediction method.

2.10 Chapter Summary

This chapter reviewed FER datasets, adult-to-child transfer, PEFT and age-adversarial adaptation. The literature shows that adult-trained models can degrade on child faces, that landmark fusion and domain adaptation can help, and that PEFT is effective in several FER settings. It also shows that CAFE is small and posed, that cross-dataset comparisons confound age with capture conditions, and that age-adversarial training can remove useful emotion information. These findings support a subject-disjoint RAF-DB-to-CAFE benchmark, a controlled LoRA vs full fine-tuning comparison, and a class-conditional age-aware adapter evaluated with an age probe. The next chapter will translate these decisions into a detailed methodology.
